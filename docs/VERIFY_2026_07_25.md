## What did NOT clear, and why

`body_fatigue` still sat at **0.988–0.999** through the idle window, with 31
`welfare_recovery_required_before_action` deferrals. The fatigue and
recovery-debt ratchet fixes are in this build and did not move it.

That failure is what found the real root. Isolation measurement:
proportional recovery drains a saturated fatigue **0.99 → 0.82 in twelve
seconds** of ordinary snapshot traffic. Decay was never the binding
constraint — so something was charging at almost exactly the decay rate, which
is a suspicious coincidence unless the charge is *derived from* the decay.

It is. `WelfareTransaction` publishes `actual_body_cost` as `b_delta`, the
**observed** change in body state across the transaction — fatigue that was
already charged. `_on_consequence` fell back to the event's unique id when no
Will receipt was present, so the idempotency guard could never fire, and that
observed fatigue was charged again as new fatigue. Bigger next delta, bigger
next charge. A loop that tracks the decay rather than competing with it.

Both earlier retunes (0.002 → 0.01, then flat → proportional) were adjusting
the wrong side of an equation whose other side was reading its own output.

Fixed in `a9720fe5b`: no receipt, no charge — an observation is not an
authorization. Authorized costs still charge exactly once and failure
consequences still register their toll. **Not yet verified live.**

The diagnostic that made it findable is worth keeping: `BodyStateService`
now holds a bounded per-source charge ledger (`charge_attribution()`), logged
once when fatigue sits at or above 0.95 and re-armed below 0.80.

# The pattern, and why it kept recurring

Nineteen defects closed in this pass. Sixteen of them are the same mistake:

> **A deliberate deferral, a designed handoff, a resting state, or a refusal,
> counted as damage.**

It appeared as: healthy admission backpressure recorded as a degradation; a
15-second recovery handoff counted as a stuck-load kill; a resident 32B's
normal footprint read as an approach to OOM; a Will deferral charging fatigue,
which fed the exhaustion that caused the deferral; a queued swarm shard
reported as a broken cognitive engine; a timeout that lost its race killing a
healthy worker; a settled lease release fencing the lane; a turn served by the
fallback ladder opening an incident.

Each instance was locally reasonable. The failure was systemic: **the runtime
had no shared way to say "this is the mechanism working."** Every subsystem
invented its own, and each one defaulted to treating an absence — no reply, no
release, no output, no message — as evidence of failure.

Three lessons worth keeping:

**An absence is not evidence.** A bare `asyncio.TimeoutError` carries no
message; an empty shard result means "not run yet" as often as "ran and
failed"; a missing lease owner means "already released" as often as "lost".
Every one of those was read as damage because the code looked at the empty
thing rather than at the caller's own account of what it did.

**Measure the regulator, not just the regulated.** Three correct fixes to the
welfare chain changed nothing live, because the fourth problem was that the
estimator driving them read 0.0017/s against a real 0.026/s load. The
charge-attribution ledger — built specifically because guessing had failed
three times — found it in one reading. Instrumentation earned its cost.

**A fix at the wrong altitude generates its own bugs.** Enumerating timeout
reasons by name missed `endpoint_timeout` within hours. Preserving the fallback
ladder unconditionally starved the cortex it was protecting. Both corrections
are in this pass; both were found by the same live run that found the original
defects, which is the argument for running the thing rather than reasoning
about it.
